Inflation: Why Prices Rise and Who Gets Hurt Most

Economics in Plain Words · 4 min read · ThinkLab

Everyone notices when groceries cost more than last year. That general rise in prices is called inflation. But the same inflation can feel mild to one family and painful to another. Here is why.

What inflation means

Inflation is the rate at which prices rise over time. If inflation is 5%, a basket of goods that cost 100 a year ago now costs about 105. Your money buys a little less than before.

A small, steady amount of inflation (around 2% a year) is normal and is what most central banks aim for. The trouble starts when prices rise much faster than incomes. In the United States, for example, inflation peaked at about 9% in mid-2022, the highest in four decades.

What makes prices rise?

  1. Too much spending, too few goods. When people and governments want to buy more than businesses can produce, sellers raise prices.
  2. Higher costs. When oil, wages or shipping get more expensive, businesses pass those costs on to customers.
  3. Expectations. If everyone expects prices to rise, workers ask for higher pay and firms raise prices early, which keeps inflation going.

Why it hurts some families more

The answer is in what people spend their money on. Lower-income families spend a much bigger part of their budget on basics like food, fuel and rent. These are often the prices that rise fastest, and you cannot simply stop buying them.

Two more groups are hit hard:

  • People with savings in cash. Money sitting in a drawer or a low-interest account loses value every year.
  • People on fixed incomes. Pensions or wages that do not rise with prices buy less and less.

Borrowers can actually gain a little: if you owe a fixed amount, inflation makes that debt smaller in real terms.

Key idea: Inflation is one number, but each family has its own inflation rate, depending on what it buys. For poorer households, it is usually higher.

How governments fight it

The main tool is the interest rate. When the central bank raises it, borrowing gets more expensive, so people and businesses spend less, and price pressure cools. The side effect is slower growth, which is why central banks try to act carefully. We will explain interest rates in a future post.
